06 / Why Facilities Planning

From Building Projects to Planning Them

Facilities planning starts before construction does: defining what a space needs to do, estimating it, scheduling it and lining up the people who will deliver it. That front end of the work is where I've been heading.

As an assistant project manager I built preliminary budgets and schedules, compared bids, approved submittals and kept owners, architects, engineers and trades aligned across five to ten projects at a time. In my current role I work with clients to define requirements and prepare preliminary estimates and proposals.

I haven't worked on laboratory or cleanroom projects, and I know those environments carry specialized requirements I would need to learn. What I bring is a working understanding of how projects are planned, documented, bought and built, plus a clear record of closing the technical gaps that were identified.

07 / Professional Approach

How I Work

  • Principle A

    Plan with the end result in mind

    Start from what the space has to do and who will use it, then build scope, budget and schedule from there.

    In practiceDefining requirements and scope with clients before preparing an estimate or proposal.

  • Principle B

    Keep budgets, schedules and documentation aligned

    A scope change should show up in cost, schedule and the project record at the same time.

    In practiceDocumenting revisions through change orders, tracking cost against budget, and reporting schedule to ownership.

  • Principle C

    Communicate early and resolve issues proactively

    Raise a discrepancy while it's still inexpensive to fix, and make sure someone owns the answer.

    In practiceChecking drawings against existing conditions, issuing RFIs, and reviewing RFI and change-order trends (15–25 per project) for design issues.

  • Principle D

    Keep developing the technical skills

    Better projects come from people who can read, mark up and eventually model the documents they coordinate.

    In practiceOSHA 30 in July, Bluebeam Revu 21 training in October, AutoCAD and Revit under way.

My role
Field Supervisor, Vega General Construction

  1. Project overview

    A two-story interior tenant improvement of roughly 6,500 square feet for a school and community facility in San Diego, with a construction value of approximately $1.5 million. It was the largest project I worked on as Field Supervisor at Vega General Construction.

  2. Scope and objectives

    Interior construction across two floors inside an existing building, including MEP work. The goal was to build the space to the design documents while handling the differences between those documents and what was actually on site.

  3. My responsibilities

    • Ran day-to-day site execution and sequenced trades, including MEP
    • Reviewed construction drawings against existing site conditions and submitted RFIs to the design team
    • Worked with vendors on pricing, delivery schedules and contract terms
    • Validated field progress against contract scope before subcontractor invoices were approved
    • Managed punch lists and enforced OSHA safety standards

  4. Coordination and planning challenges

    Tenant improvements inside an existing building rarely match the drawings exactly, and on two floors every discrepancy affects sequencing on both levels. MEP trades, framing and finishes had to be ordered so that no trade was waiting on another, and material deliveries had to arrive when each floor was ready for them.

  5. How the work was managed

    When drawings and site conditions differed, I documented the condition and sent it to the design team as an RFI before the affected work went ahead. I lined up vendor delivery dates with the trade sequence and checked completed work against contract scope before invoices were approved.

    As on my other Vega projects, I reviewed RFI and change-order trends (typically 15 to 25 per project) to spot design issues that were starting to affect schedule and cost.

  2. Scope and objectives

    Design and construction were delivered by one team. The aim was to take the design through construction without losing the intent of the drawings when they met real site conditions.

    • Reviewed construction drawings against existing site conditions
    • Submitted RFIs proposing layout and design changes where the site required them

  5. How the work was managed

    Where site conditions didn't support the drawn layout, I proposed changes to the design team through RFIs rather than adjusting informally in the field. Approved changes went to the crews as clear daily direction, and trades were sequenced so each one had the space it needed.
